Our Lady of Guadalupe — Cronologia

Chronology of the Guadalupe event in its setting: New Spain before 1531, the documentary record, the Church's judgments, the shrines, and what has been reported at Tepeyac since (1521–2025)

An open, source-referenced chronology of Our Lady of Guadalupe: the evangelisation of New Spain before 1531, the apparitions reported at Tepeyac in December 1531, the documentary record, the historicity debate, the successive shrines, the Church's judgments, and the miracles reported there — recorded, not resolved.

How far the case went

The apparitions reported at Tepeyac in December 1531 predate the Church's apparition-investigation procedure entirely. There was no local inquiry, diocesan verdict and Roman referral to run, and none was run: the modern procedure is a twentieth-century instrument. So the rungs below are not steps of a three-stage review that this case passed. They are the acts actually on record — an archbishop's inquiry into the devotion in 1556, a diocesan evidence-gathering exercise in 1666, and Roman acts concerning the cult and the person of Juan Diego — each cited and described for what it was. No ruling on whether the apparitions of 1531 occurred has ever been issued, and this chart does not supply one; the historicity disagreement recorded elsewhere on this page stands unresolved here.

About

Work-in-progress dataset compiled from public sources (see references). The apparitions are recorded here as REPORTED events: the dataset records who reported what and when, and what the Church ruled and when, citing the ruling document; it never asserts the supernatural claim as fact. The historicity of Juan Diego and of the apparition account is a recorded disagreement in this dataset, not a resolved one — named scholars on both sides are cited. Events before 1531 (the conquest, the first Franciscans, Zumárraga's arrival) are CONTEXT for the setting the account presupposes; they are not offered as corroboration of it. Reported miracles are handled the same way as everything else contested: the datable thing is the ACCOUNT — that a cure or wonder was reported, by whom, and when it was first attested — or the RECOGNITION ACT, a dated Church judgment with a document. No cure is recorded as having happened, aggregate figures and pilgrimage counts are attributed to whoever publishes them, and claims about the image's origin or preservation are attributed to the named researchers who made them. Dates not yet verified against a primary source are flagged with a ? mark. Corrections against primary sources are welcome.

What it is
A Marian devotion centred on an image of the Virgin Mary preserved on the cloak (tilma) of Juan Diego Cuauhtlatoatzin, venerated at the Basilica of Our Lady of Guadalupe at Tepeyac, Mexico City, and associated with apparitions reported there in December 1531.[6]
Reported apparitions
9–12 December 1531 (Julian calendar), at the hill of Tepeyac near Mexico City, to Juan Diego Cuauhtlatoatzin — as narrated in the Nican Mopohua. These are reported events: the narrative's earliest known printed attestations date from 1648 (Spanish) and 1649 (Nahuatl).[1] [6] [8]
The documentary record
Nican Mopohua (attributed to Antonio Valeriano; printed in Luis Laso de la Vega's Huei tlamahuiçoltica, 1649), Miguel Sánchez's Imagen de la Virgen María (1648), the Informaciones Jurídicas de 1666, and the disputed Codex Escalada (surfaced 1995). The skeptical case rests above all on the silence of the 16th-century record, including Bishop Zumárraga's surviving papers and the 1556 Bustamante–Montúfar controversy.[2] [3] [13] [4] [8]
Church judgments
Benedict XIV declared the patronage over New Spain and approved the 12 December feast (1754); the image was canonically crowned (1895); John Paul II beatified Juan Diego (1990), extended the 12 December feast to the whole American continent (1999), and canonized him on 31 July 2002.[6] [18] [17] [19]
The historicity debate
Whether Juan Diego existed and whether the apparition account predates the mid-17th century is disputed among scholars, including Catholic priests and officials: Stafford Poole, Edmundo O’Gorman and former basilica abbot Guillermo Schulenburg have argued against historicity; the historical commission of the cause — Fidel González Fernández, Eduardo Chávez Sánchez and José Luis Guerrero Rosado — the Congregation for the Causes of Saints, and scholars cited by L’Osservatore Romano, defended it. This dataset records the disagreement and attributes each position; it does not resolve it.[8] [11] [13] [20]
Reported miracles
Miracles have been reported at Tepeyac since the sixteenth century — Bernal Díaz del Castillo already writes of miracles done 'every day' there in the 1560s, and the Nican Motecpana printed in 1649 sets out fourteen. This dataset records such reports as reports: who claimed what, and when the claim is first attested. It records no cure as having happened. The one Church recognition act on this record is the decree of 20 December 2001 on a miracle attributed to Blessed Juan Diego's intercession, which belongs to his canonization cause and judges an intercession, not the apparitions. Claims about the image itself — its preservation, its pigments, reflections in its eyes — are attributed to the researchers who made them and to the date they made them.[26] [2] [33] [31]

Chronology

1520s
1521 1521-08-13 Tenochtitlan (Mexico City) Fall of Tenochtitlan — After a siege that began in May, the Mexica capital fell to Hernán Cortés and his indigenous allies and Cuauhtémoc was captured; over the following three years the conquerors brought Mesoamerica under Spanish rule and established New Spain. This is the political setting of the decade in which the apparitions are reported, recorded here as context. It is not evidence for or against the apparition account, and no source cited in this dataset connects the two events.[23]
1524 1524-05-13 San Juan de Ulúa, Veracruz The first twelve Franciscans land in New Spain — Twelve Franciscans led by Fray Martín de Valencia — later called the 'twelve apostles of Mexico' — landed at San Juan de Ulúa and walked to Mexico City, beginning the organised evangelisation of central New Spain. Their convents, catechisms in indigenous languages and mass baptisms are the missionary setting the apparition narrative presupposes; the narrative itself names none of them.[24]
1528 1528-12 ? Mexico City Zumárraga arrives as bishop-elect of Mexico and Protector of the Indians — Juan de Zumárraga, presented for the new see of Mexico on 12 December 1527, reached Mexico City at the end of 1528 carrying the title of Protector of the Indians but not yet his bulls: in December 1531 he was still bishop-elect, and he was not consecrated bishop until 27 April 1533, at San Francisco de Valladolid in Spain. This dataset therefore calls him bishop-elect in the 1531 entries. He died in Mexico on 3 June 1548. Recorded as context for who held the office the narrative places Juan Diego before; it is not an argument about whether that meeting happened.[25]The Franciscan encyclopedia gives 'fines de 1528' for his arrival; other accounts give 6 December 1528. Tepeyac, near Mexico City First reported apparition to Juan Diego — According to the Nican Mopohua narrative, the Virgin Mary appeared to Juan Diego Cuauhtlatoatzin at dawn on the hill of Tepeyac and asked that a shrine be built there; the bishop-elect, Juan de Zumárraga, is said to have received him with reserve. The narrative reports further apparitions over the following days. 1531 1531-12-12 ? Tepeyac / Mexico City Reported final apparition and the image on the tilma — The narrative reports that Juan Diego gathered roses on Tepeyac at the Virgin's instruction and opened his cloak (tilma) before Bishop-elect Zumárraga, revealing the image of the Virgin now venerated at the basilica. The image's existence and veneration are documented from the mid-16th century; that it originated miraculously on 12 December 1531 is the tradition's claim, first printed in 1648–1649.[1] [5] [6]Julian-calendar date internal to the narrative and unattested by any contemporary source; 12 December is the feast the Church later fixed (1754), which is documented.
1540s
1548 1548-05-30 ? Tepeyac, Mexico City Reported death of Juan Diego — Tradition places Juan Diego's death in 1548, the same year Bishop Zumárraga died. Skeptical scholars note that Zumárraga's surviving papers nowhere mention Juan Diego or the apparitions — one of the primary anchors of the case against historicity; defenders reply that the documentary loss of the period was severe and that the cult's indigenous transmission was oral.[7] [15] [10] [13]Britannica gives 30 May 1548 and the Vatican biography 1548; no contemporary record of Juan Diego's death is known. The only document claimed to record it, the Codex Escalada, is itself of disputed authenticity.
1550s
1556 1556-09-08 Mexico City Bustamante's sermon against the Tepeyac cult; Montúfar's inquiry — The Franciscan provincial Francisco de Bustamante preached against the growing devotion at Tepeyac — days after Archbishop Alonso de Montúfar had preached in its favour — reportedly saying the image had been painted by an Indian. Montúfar opened a formal inquiry (the Información de 1556, rediscovered and published in the 19th century). This is the earliest substantial documentary reference to the image and cult, and it mentions neither Juan Diego nor the apparitions: skeptical scholars, following Edmundo O'Gorman and Stafford Poole, treat 1555–1556 as the real horizon of the devotion; defenders read the controversy as evidence the cult was already strong 25 years after the reported events.[4] [8] [10]
1560s
1568 1568 ? Tepeaquilla (Tepeyac), New Spain Bernal Díaz del Castillo reports miracles at the Tepeaquilla chapel — In his Historia verdadera de la conquista de la Nueva España, the conquistador Bernal Díaz del Castillo writes of 'the holy chapel of Our Lady of Guadalupe, which is in Tepeaquilla' and of 'the holy miracles she has done and does every day'. The datable thing here is the REPORT: a named eyewitness of the conquest, writing in the 1560s, attests that miracles were being reported at the shrine. He describes no cure, names no beneficiary, and mentions neither Juan Diego nor any apparition. This dataset records that the claim was made and by whom; it does not record that miracles occurred.[26] [5]Bernal Díaz completed his manuscript in the 1560s — 1568 is the year conventionally given and the one the Catholic Encyclopedia uses — but the work was first printed in Madrid in 1632, and the text as printed is the text available. Flagged because the interval between composition and printing is not documented here.
1620s
1622 1622 Tepeyac, Mexico City The second shrine at Tepeyac is dedicated — Archbishop Juan Pérez de la Serna consecrated a new sanctuary at Tepeyac, the 'iglesia artesonada', replacing the small early chapel that had served the devotion for roughly ninety years. The building history of the site is one of the few strands of this record documented continuously from the sixteenth century onward, independently of the apparition question.[5] [27]
1640s
1648 1648 Mexico City Miguel Sánchez publishes Imagen de la Virgen María — The priest Miguel Sánchez published Imagen de la Virgen María, Madre de Dios de Guadalupe — the earliest known printed account of the apparitions, 117 years after the reported events. For skeptical scholars this gap is the central problem of the tradition; for defenders Sánchez merely fixed in print an account transmitted orally and in now-lost manuscripts.[3] [6] [8]
1649 1649 Mexico City Laso de la Vega publishes the Huei tlamahuiçoltica, containing the Nican Mopohua — Luis Laso de la Vega, vicar of the Guadalupe chapel, published the Nahuatl tract Huei tlamahuiçoltica, whose core narrative, the Nican Mopohua ('Here it is told'), is widely though not unanimously attributed to the indigenous scholar Antonio Valeriano (c. 1531–1605). Defenders, citing Edmundo O'Gorman among others, date Valeriano's composition to c. 1556; skeptics attribute the work substantially to Laso de la Vega himself. Manuscript copies survive, notably in the New York Public Library's Monumentos Guadalupanos collection.[2] [1] [13]
1660s
1666 1666 Mexico City / Cuautitlán Informaciones Jurídicas de 1666 — A formal ecclesiastical inquiry (February–March 1666) collected sworn testimonies — including from elderly indigenous witnesses of Cuautitlán who affirmed knowledge of Juan Diego through their parents' generation — to support a petition to Rome for a proper Mass and Office of Guadalupe. Defenders present the Informaciones as juridical evidence of a continuous tradition; skeptics note the testimonies were taken 135 years after the reported events.[13] [8]
1700s
1709 1709-05-01 Tepeyac, Mexico City The image is moved to the new shrine — today the Antigua Basílica — The larger sanctuary begun in 1695 to designs by Pedro de Arrieta was dedicated on 27 April 1709 and received the image on 1 May. It housed it for 267 years, until 1976. The colegiata was raised to the rank of basilica in 1904.[27] [5] [6]The Archdiocese of Mexico gives the first stone as 25 March 1695, completion in April 1709, dedication on 27 April 1709 and the transfer of the image on 1 May 1709. The event is dated here at the transfer.
1730s
1737 1737-04-27 Mexico City Mexico City swears Our Lady of Guadalupe as its patroness during the matlazáhuatl epidemic — The matlazáhuatl epidemic reached Mexico City late in 1736 and killed tens of thousands there over the following year. As it receded, the city swore the Guadalupan patronage before the archbishop-viceroy: Rodríguez Navarijo's diary records the act in a single line on 27 April. That the Virgin ended the epidemic is a claim, and this dataset attributes it: it is the argument of Cayetano de Cabrera y Quintero's Escudo de armas de México (1746), a work written to make exactly that case, and the same diarist who records the oath gives the intervention no such weight. Britannica notes the same credit as something Guadalupe 'was credited with'.[28] [6]The date is taken from the diary of Ignacio Rodríguez Navarijo, a contemporary witness, as published by UNAM's Instituto de Investigaciones Históricas; some accounts give 24 April instead. The disagreement is recorded, not resolved.
1740s
1746 1746 New Spain The patronage is accepted across the territories of New Spain — The patronage sworn by Mexico City in 1737 was accepted by all the territories of New Spain in 1746 — the step that made the subsequent Roman confirmation possible. Benedict XIV approved the patronage and granted the proper Mass and Office in 1754.[6]
1750s
1754 1754-05-25 ? Rome Benedict XIV declares the Guadalupan patronage of New Spain — Pope Benedict XIV declared Our Lady of Guadalupe patroness of New Spain and approved a proper Mass and Office for the feast of 12 December — the first major papal act in the Guadalupe record and the start of the Church-judgment spine this chronology follows.[6]The year 1754 is verified (Britannica); the day 25 May, widely given for the bull Non est equidem, is not yet verified here against the bull's text.
1810s
1810 1810 New Spain / Mexico Guadalupe becomes an insurgent emblem — Miguel Hidalgo y Costilla promoted Our Lady of Guadalupe as patroness of the revolt he led against Spanish rule in 1810; a century later Emiliano Zapata's peasant rebels carried her banner into Mexico City in 1914. The image's career as a national and political symbol runs alongside its devotional one and is part of what historians analyse in the 1895 coronation.[6] [19]The year is verified. The tradition that the standard was taken from the sanctuary of Atotonilco on 16 September 1810 is widely repeated and is disputed in its details; no source consulted for this dataset documents that day, so it is not recorded here.
1890s
1895 1895-10-12 Mexico City Canonical coronation of the image — The image was solemnly crowned at Tepeyac by Archbishop Próspero María Alarcón with pontifical authorization granted under Leo XIII, amid nine days of celebrations in which the Mexican hierarchy presented Guadalupe as a foundational symbol of the nation — a reading historian Jorge Traslosheros analyses as the deliberate 'updating' of a national myth.[19]
1920s
1921 1921-11-14 Old Basilica of Guadalupe, Mexico City A bomb explodes at the altar; the image is reported undamaged — Shortly after 10 a.m. a device concealed in flowers exploded at the altar of the old basilica, wrecking the altar's marble base, toppling candelabra, shattering glass and bending a bronze crucifix. The explosion is documented. That the image and even its protective glass were left intact is what contemporaries reported and what El Universal reported the following day, 15 November 1921; devotional accounts have read the crucifix — kept at the basilica as the 'Cristo del Atentado' — as having shielded it. This dataset records the explosion as an event and the intactness of the image as a report, attributed to those who made it.[29]
1930s
1939 1939 ? Mexico City / Rome Beatification process for Juan Diego initiated — According to Stafford Poole, the process that would lead to Juan Diego's beatification began in 1939. From the outset the cause was entangled with the historicity question, since the candidate's existence rests on the same contested documentary record as the apparitions.[9]Stafford Poole dates the initiation of the beatification process to 1939; not yet verified against the cause's own documents.
1970s
1976 1976-10-12 Tepeyac, Mexico City The new basilica is opened and the image transferred — The new Basilica of Santa María de Guadalupe, built from 1974 to designs by Pedro Ramírez Vázquez, José Luis Benlliure, Alejandro Schoenhofer, Fray Gabriel Chávez de la Mora and Javier García Lascuráin, received the image in solemn procession on 12 October 1976; the eighteenth-century building it replaced had become structurally unsafe as the ground beneath it subsided. The circular plan seats about ten thousand.[30] [27]
1980s
1981 1981 Washington, D.C. / Mexico City Claims about the tilma and the image are published by named researchers — The biophysicist Philip Serna Callahan published The Tilma under Infra-Red Radiation as monograph no. 3 of the CARA Studies on Popular Devotion (Georgetown University, 1981), reporting infrared photography of the cloth and arguing that parts of the image show no underdrawing or sizing. Separately, the engineer José Aste Tönsmann (1931–2024) worked on digitised enlargements of the image's eyes from 1979 and asserted that thirteen human figures are reflected in them. Both are CLAIMS by named individuals, dated and attributed here — neither was published in a peer-reviewed scientific journal, both are rejected by critics as over-reading, and this dataset takes no position on either. No Church act recorded in this chronology rules on the origin or composition of the image.[31] [32]
1990s
1990 1990-05-06 Basilica of Guadalupe, Mexico City John Paul II beatifies Juan Diego — During his second visit to Mexico, John Paul II beatified Juan Diego at the basilica — formally a recognition of his longstanding cult. The beatification intensified rather than settled the scholarly dispute over his existence.[16] [15] [7]
1995 1995 ? Mexico City The Codex Escalada surfaces — authenticity disputed — A parchment bearing the date 1548, depicting an apparition and referring to Juan Diego's death, was made known through the Jesuit Xavier Escalada. Proponents cite expert examinations — Charles E. Dibble and the Bank of Mexico's document office endorsed the signature of Bernardino de Sahagún, and a 1997 UNAM team found materials consistent with the 16th century. Skeptics — Stafford Poole calls it 'most probably a crude nineteenth century forgery', and Rafael Tena raised objections from provenance, orthography and Sahagún's documented hostility to the Tepeyac cult — reject it, noting also its timing amid the canonization debate. Both positions are recorded here; neither is adopted.[12] [14]The parchment is commonly reported as surfacing in 1995 and being published in 1997 in an appendix to Escalada's Enciclopedia Guadalupana; the find's provenance is itself part of the dispute.
1996 1996 Mexico City Abbot Schulenburg's statements and resignation — Guillermo Schulenburg, abbot of the basilica since 1963, was quoted in the Catholic magazine Ixtus saying that Juan Diego was 'a symbol, not a reality' and that his canonization would be recognition of a cult rather than of a person's real existence. The ensuing controversy forced his resignation that year; he and other clergy later wrote to the Holy See urging that the canonization be reconsidered. His statements made the historicity debate a public, intra-Church dispute on the eve of the canonization.[11] [10]
1999 1999 Mexico City / Rome The cause’s historical commission publishes its findings — The historical commission appointed for Juan Diego’s cause — presided by Fidel González Fernández, with postulator Eduardo Chávez Sánchez and vice-postulator José Luis Guerrero Rosado — published El encuentro de la Virgen de Guadalupe y Juan Diego (Porrúa, 1999), a 500-plus-page marshalling of indigenous and Spanish documents presented as establishing Juan Diego’s historical existence. It is the defenders’ principal scholarly statement; skeptics, Stafford Poole foremost, contested its readings of those documents. This chronology records the claim and the objection, and adjudicates neither.[20] [13] [9]The year of publication is verified (El Universal, 22 January 2002); the day sometimes given, 24 August 1999, is not verified here.
1999 1999-01-22 Mexico City Ecclesia in America: the feast extended to the whole continent — In the post-synodal exhortation Ecclesia in America, signed at the Guadalupe basilica, John Paul II endorsed the celebration of Our Lady of Guadalupe, 'Mother and Evangelizer of America', throughout the continent on 12 December, invoking her as Patroness of all America.[17]
2000s
2001 2001-12-20 Rome The Holy See promulgates a decree on a miracle attributed to Blessed Juan Diego — The Holy See bulletin of 20 December 2001 lists among the decrees promulgated that day one recognising 'un miracolo, attribuito all'intercessione del Beato Juan Diego Cuauhtlatoatzin' — the act that cleared the way for the canonization seven months later. This is the recognition act, and it is the datable thing: a Church judgment with a document and a date. The healing it concerns is reported in the press as that of Juan José Barragán Silva in Mexico City in May 1990; the bulletin itself names no beneficiary, and this dataset does not assert the cure. A decree of this kind judges a claimed intercession in a canonization cause; it is not a ruling on the apparitions of 1531.[33]
2002 2002-07-31 Basilica of Guadalupe, Mexico City Canonization of Juan Diego Cuauhtlatoatzin — John Paul II canonized Juan Diego at the basilica as the first indigenous saint of the Americas. The canonization itself became part of the historicity dispute: months earlier, letters from Schulenburg and other clergy questioning Juan Diego’s existence had been leaked to the press, and Stafford Poole published ‘Did Juan Diego Exist?’ weeks before the ceremony; the Congregation for the Causes of Saints and the cause’s historical commission under Fidel González Fernández, together with scholars writing in L’Osservatore Romano, defended the historical basis of the cause. This chronology records the ruling and the dispute; it adjudicates neither.[18] [7] [9] [13] [20]
2020s
2025 2025-12-12 Tepeyac, Mexico City Pilgrimage counts for the 12 December feast, as published by the counting authorities — For the feast of 2025 the Mexico City government's Secretaría de Gobierno reported, through its Operativo Basílica, close to 13 million people at the Villa de Guadalupe over 11–12 December, which it called a record against about 12.5 million for 2024. The figure is the city government's crowd estimate and is attributed to it here; the Archdiocese of Mexico republished it rather than issuing its own. The city's tourism office separately publishes an annual figure of roughly 30 million religious and 10 million other visitors, without stating a method. The counts are not reconciled here because their counters do not reconcile them.[34] [30]

Key figures

Juan Diego Cuauhtlatoatzin

1474?–1548? · Mexico (New Spain)

The reported visionary of Tepeyac: an indigenous convert (traditional dates 1474–1548) said to have received the apparitions of December 1531. Beatified 1990, canonized 2002. Whether he existed at all is the core of the historicity debate: skeptics note no document names him before 1648; the Vatican biography and the cause's defenders hold his existence proven by tradition, the 1666 testimonies and the disputed Codex Escalada.[15] [7] [8]

Juan de Zumárraga

1468–1548 · Spain / Mexico (New Spain)

Franciscan first bishop (later archbishop) of Mexico, the prelate to whom the narrative says the tilma was presented in 1531. His surviving papers nowhere mention Juan Diego or the apparitions — the most cited single anchor of the skeptical case; defenders answer that he was bishop-elect in 1531, that much of his archive is lost, and that episcopal silence about an indigenous devotion is unremarkable for the period.[6] [10] [13]

Antonio Valeriano

c. 1531–1605 · Mexico (New Spain)

Indigenous Nahua scholar of Azcapotzalco, collaborator of Sahagún and long-time judge-governor of México-Tenochtitlan; the most commonly proposed author of the Nican Mopohua (a composition defenders date to c. 1556). The attribution is traditional and debated — 'nobody knows who composed it' is the sober scholarly position.[1] [13]

Luis Laso de la Vega

fl. 1649 · Mexico (New Spain)

Vicar of the Guadalupe chapel at Tepeyac; published the Nahuatl Huei tlamahuiçoltica (1649) containing the Nican Mopohua. Skeptical scholarship regards him as substantially the author or editor of the account he printed.[2] [8]

Miguel Sánchez

1594?–1674 · Mexico (New Spain)

Mexico City priest whose Imagen de la Virgen María (1648) is the earliest known printed account of the apparitions, framing Guadalupe within a providential theology of Mexico (the Woman of Revelation 12).[3]

Alonso de Montúfar

1489–1572 · Spain / Mexico (New Spain)

Dominican second archbishop of Mexico; favoured the Tepeyac devotion and ordered the 1556 Información into Bustamante's sermon — the record of which is the earliest substantial documentation of the cult.[4]

Francisco de Bustamante

† 1562 · Spain / Mexico (New Spain)

Franciscan provincial who preached against the Tepeyac cult on 8 September 1556, reportedly saying the image was painted by an Indian — the founding document of the skeptical case, recorded in Montúfar's Información.[4]

Edmundo O'Gorman

1906–1995 · Mexico

Mexican historian whose Destierro de sombras (1986) reconstructed the 1556 controversy and argued the cult's documented origin lies in the 1550s; his work grounds much of the modern critical historiography, while defenders cite his dating of Valeriano's authorship to c. 1556 in support of an early written account.[4] [13]

Stafford Poole

1930–2020 · United States

Vincentian priest and historian; his Our Lady of Guadalupe (1995) and ‘History versus Juan Diego’ (2005) argue that no reliable 16th-century evidence connects the Tepeyac cult to the apparition account or to Juan Diego, and that the Codex Escalada is probably a forgery. The most frequently cited scholar of the skeptical side in the sources gathered here — himself a Catholic priest. Born 6 March 1930; died 1 November 2020.[8] [9] [12] [21]

Fidel González Fernández

1943– · Spain / Italy / Mexico

Comboni priest and Church historian; president of the historical commission the Congregation for the Causes of Saints charged with examining the evidence for Juan Diego. With the cause’s postulator Eduardo Chávez Sánchez and vice-postulator José Luis Guerrero Rosado he published El encuentro de la Virgen de Guadalupe y Juan Diego (Porrúa, 1999), the defenders’ documentary case; skeptics including Stafford Poole rejected its handling of the sources. The most prominent named scholars on the pro-historicity side.[20] [13]

Guillermo Schulenburg

1916–2009 · Mexico

Abbot of the Basilica of Guadalupe 1963–1996; publicly doubted Juan Diego's existence ('a symbol, not a reality', Ixtus interview, 1996) and later joined letters to the Holy See against the canonization; forced to resign in 1996. His case shows the historicity dispute running inside the Church itself.[11] [10]

Xavier Escalada

1913–2006 · Spain / Mexico

Jesuit Guadalupan scholar who published the Codex Escalada (1997) as 16th-century documentary proof of Juan Diego and the apparitions; the codex is named after him. Its authenticity is defended by the examinations he commissioned and disputed by skeptical scholars.[12] [14]

Related organizations

Basílica de Nuestra Señora de Guadalupe (Tepeyac)

Mexico City, Mexico

The shrine that holds and displays the tilma image; successive chapels and churches have stood at Tepeyac since the mid-16th century, and the present basilica was consecrated in 1976. One of the most visited Marian shrines in the world. Its own abbot (Schulenburg) became a protagonist of the historicity dispute.[6] [11] [4]

Real Monasterio de Santa María de Guadalupe (Extremadura)

Guadalupe, Cáceres, Spain

The older Spanish shrine — medieval in origin — from which the name ‘Guadalupe’ comes: it is NOT the Mexican shrine and its statue is NOT the tilma image. How the Extremaduran name attached to the Tepeyac cult (and whether early references to ‘Guadalupe’ in New Spain mean the Spanish devotion) is itself part of the historiographical debate.[8] [5]

Disambiguation & nuance

The Guadalupe record is contested terrain: the same word names two shrines on two continents, and the same documents are read as proof by one side and as late constructions by the other. This dataset records reported events, documents, and Church judgments — with each contested characterization attributed to whoever makes it.

Guadalupe (Tepeyac, Mexico) ≠ Guadalupe (Extremadura, Spain)

The Mexican devotion centres on the tilma image at Tepeyac; the Spanish one on a medieval statue in Extremadura. The Mexican cult's name derives from the Spanish shrine, and 16th-century references to 'Guadalupe' in New Spain must be disambiguated case by case — a genuine trap for sources and searches.[8] [5]

A reported apparition ≠ a documented historical event

The apparitions of 9–12 December 1531 are events reported in a narrative first printed in 1648–1649. This dataset records who reported what and when (the narrative, its manuscripts, the testimonies of 1666) and what the Church ruled and when (1754, 1895, 1990, 1999, 2002) — it does not assert the supernatural claim as fact, nor its negation.[8] [13]

Nican Mopohua ≠ Huei tlamahuiçoltica

The Nican Mopohua is the core apparition narrative; the Huei tlamahuiçoltica (1649) is the larger Nahuatl tract in which it was printed, together with the Nican Motecpana (accounts of miracles) and other material. Authorship of the parts is attributed differently by different scholars.[2] [1]

Julian dates ≠ Gregorian dates

The 1531 dates are Julian-calendar dates (the Gregorian reform came in 1582). Sources rarely state which calendar they use when discussing the apparitions; this dataset gives the dates as the tradition reports them and flags the calendar question rather than silently converting.[5]

Canonization ≠ a historical-critical verdict

The 2002 canonization is a judgment of the Church about a cult and a cause, made — as skeptics like Poole and Schulenburg publicly argued at the time — over unresolved documentary objections; defenders held the cause's historical commission had answered them. Recording the ruling is not recording a resolution of the scholarly dispute, which continued after 2002.[9] [11] [13]

A reported cure ≠ a recognized miracle

Two different things are dated in this chronology. One is an ACCOUNT — that a cure or a wonder was reported, by whom, and when the report is first attested; Bernal Díaz in the 1560s and the Nican Motecpana of 1649 are accounts. The other is a RECOGNITION ACT — a dated judgment of the Church with a document behind it, such as the decree of 20 December 2001 on a miracle attributed to Blessed Juan Diego's intercession. An account is not evidence that anything happened, and a recognition act in a canonization cause is not a ruling on the apparitions of 1531. Where a claimed cure carries neither a named reporter nor a Church act, it is not in this dataset at all.[26] [2] [33]
